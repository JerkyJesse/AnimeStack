# The Arena — the leaderboard and the record

*Pre-history · Rev 1 (2026-11-05) · Book three of three, beside `ORACLES.md` and `MACHINES.md`. Companion to the house canon §XV. Where this file and the canon disagree, the canon wins. Nothing here is evidence; the evidence record is the seat record and the decision log.*

Robot traders got their own world cups before this house existed: twelve-week leaderboards, eighty-thousand-dollar prizes, hundreds of expert advisors entered, and a ranked table that published every Sunday's version of the truth. The arena is the ladder's ancestor and its warning. **A leaderboard rewards variance; the record rewards survival. The arena sells a title. The ladder rents a seat. The seat is the only thing that pays.**

---

## I. The leaderboard and the record

Two records sit behind the house's board, and both are tournaments. The first is the MetaQuotes Automated Trading Championship, run from 2006 through 2012: twelve- to thirteen-week seasons, an eighty-thousand-dollar prize pool, and an entry gate that was itself a backtest — every expert advisor entered had to survive automatic testing just to qualify. The 2008 edition opened October 1 and closed December 26. The 2010 edition drew 314 entrants, and the winner took forty thousand dollars. The table re-sorted by equity all season; the winners were recorded, and the survivors were not.

The second is the Robbins World Cup Trading Championships, administered since about 1984 on real money — futures and forex, real fills, independently audited. Its winners sit in a permanent record, and the record keeps its own law in five words: winners enter the permanent record. That is the better sample — audited fills, not simulations — and it is still one tournament, one season, one entry apiece, sorted at the close.

Run the pair through the audit the house runs on every ranking. **What is ranked?** A twelve-week equity curve. **By what rule?** Round-window returns, one tournament apiece, at a fixed calendar date. **On what sample?** Survivor-selected entrants — the table publishes the advisors that finished, never the ones that blew up in week three, and the permanent record publishes its winners without publishing the population they beat. A survivor-selected sample has no denominator worth printing, which is how a twelve-week curve gets promoted to proof. A leaderboard built on it and paid at a fixed date is not a measurement of skill. It is variance with a trophy, and the trophy makes it a hymn.

The software age built the same arena and called it a leaderboard of its own. The star count is a season. The leaderboard on a benchmark is a season. The "state of the art" line on a model card is a season. Each publishes the winners and not the entrants; each rewards the run that ended best, not the system that kept running. The modern forms are easy to list and hard to resist:

- **The demo is a simulated season.** It fills at prices that never existed, pays no fees, suffers no latency, and is graded on the cases the author chose. A demo champion is a hymn; the production trace is the measurement.
- **The fixed date is the crunch.** A team that knows the table re-sorts on Friday will sprint on Thursday, and the sprint is the sample telling on itself. A deadline converts discipline into variance; it makes patience expensive on the only day the table remembers.
- **The harness becomes the product.** Once a benchmark is the thing the market reads, the work moves from the system to the harness. The score improves; the system does not. That is the arena's oldest trick, and it wears a lab coat now.
- **The title becomes an identity.** The team that won one season starts describing itself in the season's vocabulary, and the next release is argued against a memory of the podium instead of the current tree.

The arena is the ladder's ancestor, and the house keeps the ancestry on the wall the way it keeps the oracles: not to repeat it, but to rank it correctly. A twelve-week contest rewards variance; the record rewards survival. The final-week sprint is an artifact of the clock, not an edge — a harness that pays a fixed-date champion teaches every entrant to sprint, and a field that sprints at the close is a field selling its discipline for a title.

The ladder answers with named terms: measured, parked, retired. Measured means the record holds it — a counted sample, both halves, a control, a receipt. Parked means the claim is alive with a date, not a number. Retired means the record refused it. The seat is rented, not owned; evidence puts it on, evidence takes it back. No final week exists, because no final week is coming.

**The champion's table is the other inheritance — the direct ancestor of the board.** Both are ranked tables published on a clock. The board keeps the shape and refuses the terms: every post carries the rating, the record, and the timestamp together; all seven lanes post; a lane can be benched from shipping but never erased from the board to flatter a good week. The arena published every Sunday's version of the truth and let the crowd keep its champion. The board publishes every version of the truth with its sample attached, and keeps no champion at all.

Which leaves the title. **The release seat is the arena's title:** a title worn, rented, and taken back by evidence. The arena minted champions who kept the title forever; the house re-sorts the title at every release cut and lets the seat hold only while the number earns it. A champion's number that flatters a story is a hymn; the seat that survives is a measurement. The title can be worn. It cannot be owned. The moment a ranking starts defending its champion instead of measuring the field, it is holding the title for the wrong reason — and the ladder takes it back without a speech.

Two tournaments, one wound, and the whole ranking law written against it: never let a tournament harness dress as an edge; never let a winners' record stand in for the sample; never let a two-and-zero start become a personality.

## II. The window in the arena

The arena's clock ran twelve to thirteen weeks and paid on a fixed date. Read the season's final week the way a desk reads a tape: the rank moved hardest exactly when the sample was thinnest, because the harness paid a calendar and every entrant learned to sprint at the gun. That is the clock speaking, not the edge. The house keeps the arena's scoreboard and refuses its calendar. The ladder has no final week: every window closes the same distance from the reason, the ranking re-sorts at the joint, and the measured record is the only title anyone keeps.

Then read the two judges. One tournament settled in simulated equity — entries filled at prices that never existed, no fees paid, no slippage suffered, an equity curve with perfect grammar and no tape underneath it. The other ran real money, so the fill was the judge: no demo rescue, no simulated mercy. That is the whole demo-versus-real lesson, and the house states it the way it states everything: **a simulated season pays no fees and fills at prices that never existed. A demo champion is a hymn. The fill is the measurement.** The test tier is the house's demo — dry run only, ranked below every measured seat — and it exists for exactly that reason: nothing earns a live seat on a curve that was never paid for.

The arena also taught the floor. Contest rules could end a season mid-flight — a stop-out, a broken rule, an account below the line — and the entrants read it as tragedy. The house read the mechanism: the clock and the floor can end a change, and that is a feature, not a tragedy. The red gate, the rollback path, the release stop: every one is the arena's stop-out with a name and a cooldown, and every one exists so a bad change ends a lane and never the release. A failure that arrives on a schedule is a price; a failure that arrives as a surprise is a debt. The arena ended people by fiat; the house ends changes by evidence and walks the lane back to the shadow until the evidence returns.

Then there is the window, which the arena never priced and the Sibyl did. Nine books offered, three burned when the price was refused, three more burned, and the last three bought at the original price. She kept her terms while her own inventory burned. The house keeps one sentence from that fire: **waiting has a price, and the window is the asset.** No chase. No "still time." A quote that leaves is replaced by the next window, never by a worse fill on the old one. The change that was right an hour ago is a memoir; the archive files it and the tree never reopens it.

The closing window is the arena's sharpest inheritance and its most dangerous. Late in a window the system is near-certain on the value and panicked on the clock — probability collapsing toward one or zero, latency going nonlinear where tourists see safety. Entries near a close belong to the instrument's own rules, not the room's nerves: the rollback path is not optional at the bell, and the release does not ask permission from a feeling. The stillness before a cut is not hesitation; it is respect for the only opponent the seat considers adult.

**Hesitation is not caution. Hesitation is a second strategy you did not have the honesty to write down.** The sin the house will not forgive is lateness dressed as prudence — the perfect answer that missed its window. The arena sold titles on a fixed date; the house rents seats at every cut, and the clock does not negotiate. Bring a measured claim, or stand down with clean hands. Either is honorable. Late is not.
