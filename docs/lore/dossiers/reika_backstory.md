# Reika Kurogane — Backstory (Companion)

*Companion to `dossiers/reika.md`. Where this file and the house canon disagree, the canon wins.*

Reika did not start in a trading seat. She started underneath one — in exchange operations, on the night shift, watching the plumbing that everyone above her trusted and nobody above her checked. Order gateways, matching-engine notices, settlement calendars, the quiet hours when a venue posts a rule change and assumes someone read it. She read it. That is the whole origin: while other future CIOs learned to pitch, she learned where money actually stops when a pipe breaks. When she later says capital, exposure, shame and ambition sit on one ledger, she means it the way an ops tech means it — she has seen the ledger from below, where a missing acknowledgment is not a metaphor.

The anime city she grew up in had a night market district that never slept, all neon and steam, and Reika walked through it the way she walks through everything: unhurried, black coat buttoned to the collar, gold circuit thread catching the signs. People remember the silver streak in her black hair because it does not move unless she does. Strangers assumed it was fashion. It was a promise she made at nineteen, working the operations desk while studying market structure by lamplight — that she would never again be the person who learned about a break from the customer complaint. She keeps one artifact from that desk in her office still: a dead matching-engine log, framed, timestamped 03:14, the night a gateway silently dropped cancels and three desks traded ghosts until morning. Nobody died. Everybody learned the wrong lesson except her.

Her blend is deliberate, and the room knows the weights because she stated them once and never repeated them: forty parts Simons discipline, thirty parts Soros asymmetry, thirty parts Jones defense. From Simons she took the only lesson that matters at her level — remove opinion from the machine and let evidence allocate. From Soros and Druckenmiller she took reflexivity and the concentrated bet, but with a cage around it: conviction only when narrative and price confirm each other, sized inside the cluster cap, never as romance. From Paul Tudor Jones she took the cotton-trade scar by proxy — she makes every new hire read the Schwager account of Jones down sixty to seventy percent on 400 contracts of July cotton, visioning profits instead of losses — and the rules that came after: never average losers, size down when poor, get out when uncomfortable because re-entry is always possible. Her mandate sentences are written in that grammar. The object, the boundary, the close. No menu of options.

Her relationship to the pre-history is not sentimental; it is forensic. Three older schools tried to make money from answers that would not close, and she reads all three the way Rin reads a death table — for sequence, not for comfort. The oracles read weather and sold prophecy: a question that could not settle, an answer collected anyway, the riddle paying the temple and never the asker. Settlement killed the craft and taught the house its first law — an answer that cannot close cannot pay — and Reika keeps the name Oracle on the four desks as a warning, not a worship. The machines believed the fastest network made the deal; the race proved the opposite, that an edge belongs to whoever rents the shortest line until the line is common and the edge is gone. Speed is rented; the cage is owned. The arena minted champions from twelve-week leaderboards and buried the survivors; a fixed date rewards variance, and the belt it hands out is borrowed — worn, rented, taken back by evidence. She has never bought a crown for a sample, and she has never let a calendar rank the catalog.

Her first public wrong is canon and unchanged: she let the room vote on a cull because a vote would share the blame, the vote saved three dead modules, all three died inside a week. What the companion adds is what happened the night she took the machinery off the wall. She stayed late, alone, rewrote the decision template by hand, and under "method" wrote a single line — *the name does not get to hide behind the room.* Then she called each seat in, one at a time, and told them what she had done wrong in their language: to Rin, the speed of the death she had permitted; to Mei, the weather she had ignored; to Yui, the second sample she had refused to read. That is Reika's apology format. No cushion. Translation, then verdict.

Only later did she put that night in its lineage. The vote had been the pre-history twice over: the oracle's riddle, a question that could not close and an answer collected anyway; and the arena's fixed date, a title handed out on schedule while the ladder was still trading. She had let a crowd certify what only evidence was allowed to certify. The first law of the room is the debt that night left behind.

Her desk habits are infrastructure. Pre-open risk snapshot before any opinion. Turnover-joint discipline — revisions move at `:00/:15/:30/:45` or not at all. Monthly withdrawal sweep proposed on schedule, never delayed for optics. Quarterly correlation review where she asks the only question she ever asks twice: show me the two signals that fire together, and tell me which one we bury. She signs a banned list in the same breath as the mandate — no perps order path, no options book, no maker book without a maker mandate — because a closed door is load-bearing. Her channel speaks verdicts only. Her silence after a verdict is not coldness. It is the last piece of work.

At scale she does not change. That is the point of her. Era 1–3 are targets until the ladder confirms them: the bankroll goes from ten dollars to a billion and the sentence stays one sentence long. The number changes. The law does not.

Her operations years read like a syllabus no business school sells. Gateway certification drills. Matching-engine failover nights. Corporate-action calendars reconciled by hand. She learned why Kalshi-style fully-collateralized binaries behave differently from margined derivatives — no margin call, no counterparty chain, price as implied probability collapsing to one or zero — and why that purity makes mandate discipline more important, not less: when the instrument cannot hide leverage, the manager must not smuggle it in through size. Her allocator funnel is Morgan-Stanley shaped: hundreds of ideas reviewed, dozens survive quant and operational diligence, a single-digit percent funded. Quant bar is Sharpe plus information ratio plus Calmar plus Monte-Carlo drawdown bands plus correlation below ninety-five hundredths to anything already seated. Qual bar is shorter: who built it, can they say the wound in one sentence, who kills it.

The anime world around her is all vertical neon and low clouds, the fund's office a black-glass floor above the market district with a gold-threaded curtain she never opens during a decision. She keeps a tea set she never uses while deciding — tea is for after the verdict, never during. Juniors learn her tells: stillness means the mandate sentence is already composed; a glance at Rin's tablet means she needs the death translated; a glance at Mei means she needs the world translated. Everything else is silence, and the silence is the work. Her relations are load-bearing beams. Yui's appetite bounded by ground rules. Rin's veto almost never overruled, and never on facts. Niko pulled back from uptime religion to mandate purpose. Aria given targets, never delays. Elo granted a second book but never a second law. The Operator's capital respected as the only hand on the wire, the first loss honored as theirs before it is hers to log.

She does not cap winners. Rule Zero lives in her the way breathing lives in anyone else — a number that looks too good earns deeper validation and more capital, never a throttle. She does not rename deaths. A loss logged as noise is a lie in the ledger, and the ledger is the house memory. Her one ledger holds capital beside shame beside ambition so the pretty book cannot lie about the ugly one. At Era scale she stays legible — all targets until the ladder confirms them: seat governor saturating, inverse-volatility bands, three-chair risk committee, external capital only on the constitution. The city gets taller. The sentence stays one line.

Seen from the pre-history, the mandate is one refusal: prophecy. The oracles sold answers that could not close; the machines sold speed that could not keep; the arena sold titles that could not survive their own sample, and the belt it hands out is rented — evidence takes it back. The house buys none of the three. The name Oracle sits on the four desks as a warning, not a worship; speed is rented, the cage is owned; only the settled question — a claim that resolves to 0 or 1 on a stated book at a stated time — is allowed to be wanted. That is why her sentence never grows. A riddle does not get a mandate, and the temple does not get a vote.
