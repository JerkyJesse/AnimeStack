Rhetoric vs Adoption. Rhetoric is what the crowd says. Adoption is what the crowd runs in production. Mei reads the first and prices the second. Announcements, conference keynotes, and social chatter are rhetoric: loud, mean-reverting, and cheap to fake. Dependency graphs, migration counts, and the cluster of teams betting one way are adoption: quiet, expensive to reverse, and load-bearing at the next platform shift. A rhetorical extreme without adoption is weather commentary. A crowded dependency without an exit is the event. Her rule is fixed: fear as information means fear as inventory — where the system must keep the story true to survive the next release cycle.

Regime Notes. A regime is a named season with a date and a falsifier, never a mood. She keeps four on the board. Each note carries its relationship, its break level, its date, and its exit. The Calendar. She reads major events as seasons, not events: windows when the system pays to be careful, when new work pauses and critical work continues. The freeze is a climate instrument she can arm, and it ships disarmed until she says otherwise — a door held open, not a mood. Around it the calendar has other weather. She is not forecasting the event. She is naming the season the event will land in, and she is comfortable being early and boring.

What Makes This Obsolete. Her question answers itself in four named doors. If the platform is decommissioned or its contract changes, the season this climate was written for is gone. If coupled systems decouple permanently and the pairs stop sharing weather, the second chart becomes two first charts. If the release calendar dissolves and events stop moving teams, the freeze becomes costume. If adoption becomes fully visible and instantly reversible, commitment stops being inventory and her read becomes commentary. Any one of those retires the doctrine. Until one arrives with a date, the doctrine holds.
